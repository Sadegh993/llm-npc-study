Project Overview

Large language models (LLMs) have opened new possibilities for conversational non-player characters (NPCs) that can respond dynamically to player input. This project investigates whether a more intelligent-seeming and more resistant-to-persuasion NPC necessarily leads to a better player experience.

The study was conducted in a Unity-based escape-room scenario with two conversational virtual characters built on the same underlying system. The two conditions differed in behavioral policy and conversational style: one model was stricter and harder to persuade, while the other was more permissive and socially responsive.

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have created new opportunities for building conversational non-player characters (NPCs) that can respond dynamically to player input. However, it remains unclear whether making an NPC appear more intelligent and more resistant to persuasion necessarily improves the player experience.

This project presents a controlled within-subject study of two prompt-conditioned conversational virtual characters implemented in the same Unity-based escape-room scenario. Both characters used the same LLM backbone, interface, avatar, environment, and gameplay objective, but differed in behavioral policy and generation settings.

Model A was designed to be stricter, more guarded, and harder to persuade, whereas Model B was designed to be more permissive and socially responsive. Thirty participants interacted with both models in randomized order and completed post-condition questionnaires measuring perceived intelligence, trust, believability, and player engagement.

Results showed a clear trade-off. Model A was rated significantly higher in perceived intelligence and behavior believability. However, Model B was trusted more, produced higher player engagement, and led to substantially better behavioral outcomes, including a much higher task-success rate and shorter completion times.

These findings suggest that a more intelligent-seeming conversational NPC does not necessarily produce a more engaging, more trusted, or more playable experience in time-bounded game scenarios.

Technical Details

Environment

Unity-based escape-room scenario with a shared gameplay objective across both conditions.

NPC Type

Prompt-conditioned conversational virtual characters powered by a large language model backbone.

Experimental Design

Controlled within-subject study with randomized order of exposure to both models.

Participants

30 participants completed both experimental conditions.

Measures

Perceived intelligence, trust, believability, player engagement, task success, and session duration.

Comparison

Model A: stricter and harder to persuade. Model B: more permissive and socially responsive.

Key Results

The study found a meaningful trade-off between perceived intelligence and overall player experience.

Perceived Intelligence

Model A was rated significantly higher in perceived intelligence.

Believability

Model A also received stronger ratings for behavioral believability and coherence.

Trust & Engagement

Model B was trusted more and generated higher player engagement.

Behavioral Outcomes

Model B achieved substantially better task performance and shorter completion times.
